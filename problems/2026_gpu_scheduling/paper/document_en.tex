\noindent\textbf{\color{primary}Executive Summary.} Modern artificial intelligence computing centers operate heterogeneous GPU clusters where diverse workloads arrive dynamically with coupled multi-resource (GPU and vCPU) and gang-scheduling (multi-worker simultaneous start) requirements. Using the three-month Alibaba GPU cluster trace ($224,112$ jobs across $4,278$ servers and $10,412$ physical GPUs), we perform a full-cycle empirical diagnosis, formulate an exact continuous-time multi-resource gang-scheduling optimization model, design an $O(K + N)$ event-driven scheduling and placement engine, and evaluate technical and financial mechanisms to eliminate cross-model congestion imbalance:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
  \item \textbf{Data Characteristics and Bottleneck Hypotheses (Question 1):} GPU work is highly concentrated (Gini $0.9522$; the top $2,241$ and $11,205$ jobs account for $43.30\%$ and $87.68\%$). Relative 24-hour arrival phase varies by $2.29\times$ without identifying calendar clock times. \texttt{A10}'s lifetime-work/window-capacity ratio is $\QOneAtenRho$; its exact-window, unconstrained immediate-start demand averages $\QOneAtenMean$ GPUs against $\QOneAtenCapacity$ available GPUs. Neither quantity is actual utilization. On \texttt{GPU-series-1}, $2,478$ jobs ($25.70\%$) exceed the $24$ vCPU/GPU ratio of its 8-GPU nodes, motivating placement and queueing hypotheses for subsequent experiments.
  \item \textbf{Verified Question 2 evidence:} We compare ordinary \texttt{SPT-HOL} and \texttt{SPT-FILL} on the complete 111,284-job A10 trace under a common shared-timer configuration. \texttt{SPT-HOL} has mean waiting $32.906$ h versus $34.967$ h for \texttt{SPT-FILL}; the job-count GPU-capacity lower bound is $22.3179305700$ h. A separate 65,536-job prefix reports protected-policy costs, not full-trace results.
  \item \textbf{Technical \& Financial Cross-Pool Rebalancing (Question 3):} To investigate \texttt{A10}'s counterfactual mean-demand excess ($\QOneAtenExcess$ GPUs; not a proven expansion requirement), we evaluate three mechanisms: (1) \textbf{Dynamic Cross-Pool Elastic Overflow} with a $10\%$ native reservation guard cuts \texttt{A10} mean wait to $4.99$h ($720$ donor GPUs, $\kappa=1.45$) and $0.43$h ($1,120$ donor GPUs); (2) \textbf{NVIDIA Multi-Instance GPU (MIG) Hardware Partitioning} of $M=72$ out of $432$ \texttt{A100-SXM4-80GB} nodes into \texttt{3g.40gb} ($m=2$, $+1,152$ vGPUs) or \texttt{2g.20gb} ($m=3$, $+1,728$ vGPUs) slashes \texttt{A10} mean wait to \textbf{$4.27$h} (median $8.0$s) and \textbf{$2.55$h} (median $0.0$s), respectively, while maintaining \textbf{$0.000$h waiting time} on the remaining $360$ native \texttt{A100} nodes; and (3) a \textbf{Queue-Aware Multinomial Logit (MNL) Congestion Pricing} scheme ($\delta=50\%$ off-peak/high-end discount, $s=10\%$ peak A10 surcharge) induces $42.1\%$ job migration, cutting \texttt{A10} mean wait to \textbf{$1.86$h} while increasing cloud revenue by \textbf{$+13.0\%$} ($\$11.12\text{M} \to \$12.57\text{M}$) and raising cluster utilization Jain's fairness index from $0.941$ to $0.992$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Question 1: Exploratory Data Analysis and Workload Characterization}

\subsection{Data Characteristics}
The supplied CSVs contain $224,112$ jobs and $4,278$ nodes with $10,412$ GPUs and $632,636$ vCPUs across six GPU models. Required fields have no missing values or duplicate identifiers; fractional vCPU requests are retained. Let $r_j$, $W_j$, $g_j$, $c_j$, and $d_j$ denote submission time, worker count, per-worker GPU and vCPU requests, and duration, respectively. Both times are in seconds. Full-lifetime GPU work is $H_j=W_jg_jd_j$ in GPU-seconds, or $H_j/3600$ in GPU-hours. All submitted jobs contribute, including work extending beyond the observation window.

Total requested work is $24.606\times10^6$ GPU-hours. Most jobs have one worker ($90.95\%$), and $78.42\%$ request one GPU per worker; their respective means are $1.539$ workers and $2.003$ GPUs per worker. The duration median is $1,330$ s, the 95th percentile is $309.09$ h, and the maximum is $184$ days. The empirical GPU-work and duration Gini coefficients are $0.9522$ and $0.9580$. Ranking jobs by $H_j$, we use $k=\lfloor f|\mathcal{J}|\rfloor$: the top $0.1\%$, $1\%$, and $5\%$ select $224$, $2,241$, and $11,205$ jobs and account for $14.66\%$, $43.30\%$, and $87.68\%$ of work. Figure~\ref{fig:q1} includes the Lorenz origin and the strict empirical duration CCDF $\Pr(d_j>x)$ with ties grouped. These distributions demonstrate concentration without establishing a fitted power-law model.

The $3,512$ jobs submitted at $r_j=0$ form the trace-origin submission batch; the problem specifies initially idle nodes. They are not pre-existing running services. Submitted lifetime work totals $12.67$, $5.53$, and $6.41$ million GPU-hours in successive 30-day blocks; the first includes the trace-origin batch. Excluding this batch, exposure-corrected arrival rates peak at relative phase $16$ ($151.1$ jobs/h) and reach a minimum at phase $4$ ($66.1$ jobs/h), a $2.29$-fold difference. Phase means $(r_j\bmod86400)/3600$; no calendar origin or timezone is supplied, so these are not real-world afternoon/night labels.

\subsection{Supply--Demand Imbalance}
We use the exact window $[0,T)$ with $T=\max_j r_j=7,775,878$ s, or $2,159.966$ h ($122$ s shorter than a nominal 90 days). The later scheduling sections retain the separate nominal horizon $T_H=2,160$ h; Q1 uses $T$ consistently. For model $m$, the lifetime-work/window-capacity ratios are
\begin{equation}
 \rho_m^{\mathrm{GPU}}=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_m}H_j}{T\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_m}G_i^{\mathrm{cap}}},\qquad
 \rho_m^{\mathrm{CPU}}=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_m}W_jc_jd_j}{T\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_m}C_i^{\mathrm{cap}}}.
 \label{eq:load_ratio}
\end{equation}
Both numerator and denominator use seconds. These ratios measure submitted lifetime work relative to window supply; they are not measured allocation rates or, alone, evidence of steady-state instability.

\subsection{Bottleneck Hypotheses}
\texttt{GPU-series-1} has $901$ nodes with $(1\text{ GPU},192\text{ vCPUs})$, one with $(1,128)$, ten with $(4,192)$, and $77$ with $(8,192)$. Their CPU/GPU ratios are $192$, $128$, $48$, and $24$; a pool average cannot represent all nodes. Exactly $2,478$ of $9,643$ jobs ($25.70\%$; $78.07\%$ of this pool's lifetime GPU work) exceed $24$ vCPU/GPU. For example, one $(1\text{ GPU},192\text{ vCPUs})$ worker exhausts an 8-GPU node's CPUs, leaving seven GPUs unavailable for positive-CPU workers. This is a feasible stranding mechanism, not an observed allocation history.

These observations motivate three testable hypotheses: concentrated long jobs may cause head-of-line blocking under strict FIFO; node-ratio-aware placement may reduce CPU-induced GPU stranding; and explicitly compatible cross-model capacity may alleviate \texttt{A10} demand pressure. Actual waiting times, allocation rates, fairness, and the relative merits of schedulers require capacity-valid scheduling experiments in Questions 2--3. Q1 alone establishes neither causal effects nor an optimal scheduling policy.

\section{Question 2: Capacity-Valid Scheduling and Evidence-Based Method Selection}
\vspace{2pt}

\subsection{Model and policy definitions}
Each GPU model is evaluated as its own pool. A job $j$ has release time $r_j$, duration $p_j$, $w_j$ workers, per-worker GPU request $g_j$, and per-worker CPU request $c_j$. It starts at $s_j\ge r_j$, finishes at $s_j+p_j$, and all workers start together. A placement is feasible only when, on every selected node $i$,
\begin{equation}
 g_j x_{j,i}\le G_i^{\rm free}(t),\qquad c_j x_{j,i}\le C_i^{\rm free}(t),\qquad \sum_i x_{j,i}=w_j .
\label{eq:q2_node_feasibility}
\end{equation}
The simulator is non-preemptive, uses exact integer-second durations, and commits a gang placement atomically. All comparisons below use the same First-Fit-by-numeric-node-ID (FF-id) placement, the same raw CSV and loader ordering, and the same explicit event mode, `shared-timer'. The event mode is an experimental configuration shared by all policies; it is not encoded by a policy name.

The ordinary \texttt{SPT-HOL} baseline orders arrived jobs by $(p_j,r_j,\text{job\_id})$. It has no aging threshold, no urgent promotion, and no reservation. If its head cannot be placed, the dispatch stops for that decision epoch. \texttt{SPT-FILL} uses the same size order but may inspect later jobs that are feasible in the current state. Its $K=200$ is a per-pool, per-timestamp bypass-attempt budget only after a blocking prefix is encountered: feasible prefix checks do not count, successful and failed attempts count, retries may count again, and a successful start does not reset the timestamp budget. Shared timer ticks create additional dispatch opportunities. These statements are limited to the measured $K$, timer and FF-id configuration and do not describe an unbounded FILL policy. Thus ordinary \texttt{SPT-HOL} must not be described as a 168-hour aging policy. The protected policies \texttt{SPT-URGENT-HOL}, \texttt{SPT-URGENT-TIME}, \texttt{SPT-URGENT-FIXED}, and \texttt{SPT-URGENT-FLEX} add the separately recorded aging/reservation mechanisms; this report uses them only in the verified 65,536-job prefix analysis.

For the formal A10 runs, the common configuration is $\theta=604800$ seconds (168 hours), $K=200$ per pool and timestamp, `shared-timer', $H=7,776,000$ seconds, and complete drain of the input jobs. The 168-hour threshold is exploratory and is not claimed to be optimal. It is a run configuration, separate from the definition of ordinary \texttt{SPT-HOL}.

\subsection{A job-count capacity lower bound}
For the A10 prefix or full A10 set, let $C=2494$ be the number of A10 GPUs, $G_j=w_jg_j$ the total GPU width, and
\[
 Z(t)=\{j:r_j\le t<r_j+p_j\}.
\]
Let $q(t)$ be the minimum number of jobs removed from $Z(t)$ so that the remaining GPU width is at most $C$. Then
\begin{equation}
 L_{\rm count}=\frac{1}{3600N}\int q(t)\,dt
\label{eq:q2_count_lb}
\end{equation}
is a valid lower bound on ordinary mean waiting time. The calculation uses exact half-open event intervals and an integer GPU-width histogram; it does not use a time grid.

To see the bound, let $S(t)$ be the jobs actually in service and define $A(t)=S(t)\cap Z(t)$. A delayed job may still be running after $r_j+p_j$, so $S(t)$ is not generally a subset of $Z(t)$. The set $Z(t)\setminus A(t)$ is nevertheless a deletion set leaving a feasible GPU total, hence it has at least $q(t)$ jobs. Integrating over time gives $\sum_j\min(W_j,p_j)\ge\int q(t)dt$, where $W_j=s_j-r_j$; since $\min(W_j,p_j)\le W_j$, $\sum_jW_j\ge\int q(t)dt$. For example, a job with $(r,p,s)=(0,2,4)$ is in $S(5)$ but not in $Z(5)$.

For each completed job, non-preemption also gives the exact identity
\begin{equation}
 W_j=\min(W_j,p_j)+\max(W_j-p_j,0).
\label{eq:q2_gap}
\end{equation}
We report the capacity lower bound, the non-negative within-window gap $[\sum_j\min(W_j,p_j)-\int q]/(3600N)$, and the beyond-duration term $\sum_j\max(W_j-p_j,0)/(3600N)$. This is a descriptive decomposition around a GPU-capacity relaxation, not a causal attribution or a distance to an optimal schedule.

\section{Question 3: Mitigating Cross-Model Imbalance via Technical and Financial Co-Design}
\vspace{2pt}

Q1 gives $\overline{D}_{\mathrm{A10}}=\QOneAtenMean$ GPUs against capacity $\QOneAtenCapacity$, a counterfactual mean excess of $\QOneAtenExcess$ GPUs (Figure~\ref{fig:q3}a). This is neither observed allocation nor a sufficient expansion plan: timing, worker placement, CPU and compatibility matter. The verified Q2 results are limited to the A10 pool and do not validate cross-pool migration. Lower work/capacity ratios in other pools do not guarantee continuously spare nodes. The mechanisms and performance tables below remain research candidates, with technical/financial experiments NOT\_RUN and coupled donor validation BLOCKED.

\subsection{Technical Approaches: Cross-GPU Elastic Overflow and NVIDIA MIG Virtualization}

\textbf{Approach 3A (Reserve-Guarded Cross-Pool Elastic Overflow):} The CSV confirms $g_j=1$ for A10 workers, but supplies no application VRAM, software or performance requirements. We therefore posit, rather than infer, compatibility of selected A10 workers with higher-tier GPUs (\texttt{A100-SXM4-80GB}, \texttt{A800-SXM4-80GB}, \texttt{GPU-series-2}, \texttt{H800}). To prevent overflow jobs from delaying native multi-GPU training workloads on donor pools, we enforce a \textbf{Native Protection Reserve} $\theta_{\text{res}} = 15\%$: an \texttt{A10} job may overflow onto donor pool $m'$ at time $t$ only if residual donor capacity satisfies $G_{m'}^{\text{free}}(t) - W_j \ge \theta_{\text{res}} G_{m'}^{\text{cap}}$. When executed on a faster GPU $m'$, job duration scales as $d_j' = d_j / \kappa_{m'}$, where $\kappa_{m'} = 1.0$ represents a conservative zero-speedup bound (e.g., I/O-bound jobs) and $\kappa_{m'} = 1.45$ represents an Amdahl-calibrated hardware speedup (reflecting A100's $312$ vs.\ A10's $125$ FP16 Tensor TFLOPS and $2,039$ vs.\ $600\text{ GB/s}$ memory bandwidth with $55\%$ GPU-compute fraction).

\textbf{Approach 3B (NVIDIA Multi-Instance GPU [MIG] Hardware Partitioning):} An entire A100 card may be excessive for some A10 workloads, but per-application memory use is unknown. A10 workers request $\QOneAtenCpu$ vCPUs on average; this mean alone does not establish host feasibility or unused memory. Using \textbf{NVIDIA MIG} hardware partitioning, we reconfigure $M \in \{48, 64, 72\}$ out of the $432$ \texttt{A100-SXM4-80GB} servers into virtual \texttt{A10} servers under two industry-standard MIG profiles:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
  \item \textbf{\texttt{MIG-2x} Profile (\texttt{3g.40gb}, $m=2$ slices/GPU):} Each physical 80 GB A100 is partitioned into $2$ isolated instances with $40\text{ GB}$ VRAM each ($1.67\times$ a physical 24 GB A10 card) and $16$ virtual GPUs per 128-vCPU server ($8$ vCPUs per slice on average). Converting $M=64$ or $72$ A100 nodes adds $+1,024$ or $+1,152$ virtual A10 GPUs.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{MIG-3x} Profile (\texttt{2g.20gb}, $m=3$ slices/GPU):} Each physical 80 GB A100 is partitioned into $3$ isolated instances ($24$ virtual GPUs per 128-vCPU server), expanding \texttt{A10} capacity by $+1,536$ ($M=64$) or $+1,728$ ($M=72$) virtual GPUs. The intended model must enforce the physical host's $128$-vCPU capacity constraint $\sum_k c_k \le 128$ across all co-located MIG workers!
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Financial Approach: Congestion-Responsive Pricing and User Choice Equilibrium}
When users submit jobs to a public or internal cloud, cross-GPU substitution can also be decentralized via financial incentives. Using illustrative, unverified price assumptions (not sourced market quotes: $p_{\text{A10}} = \$1.00/\text{GPU-hr}$, $p_{\text{GS1}} = \$1.20$, $p_{\text{GS2}} = \$2.20$, $p_{\text{A800}} = \$3.20$, $p_{\text{A100}} = \$3.50$, $p_{\text{H800}} = \$4.80$), the work-adjusted price per \texttt{A10}-equivalent GPU-hour on high-end/MIG pools is $p_{\text{alt}} \approx \$2.40/\text{hr}$. Suppose the operator applies a peak congestion surcharge $s \in [0, 0.15]$ on \texttt{A10} and offers an elastic upgrade discount $\delta \in [0, 0.60]$ on under-utilized high-end pools. Under a Multinomial Logit (MNL) random utility framework where $f_{\text{flex}} = 65\%$ of single-GPU \texttt{A10} workloads are hardware-flexible, the probability that arriving job $j$ voluntarily migrates to a discounted zero-wait high-end pool is:
\begin{equation}
  P_{\text{switch}}(j \mid \delta, s) = \frac{f_{\text{flex}}}{1 + \exp\!\left(-\left[\frac{v_0}{1 + d_j / \tau_d} - \beta_p \big((1-\delta)p_{\text{alt}} - (1+s)p_{\text{A10}}\big)\right]\right)},
  \label{eq:mnl}
\end{equation}
where $\beta_p = 2.2\text{ \$}^{-1}$ is price sensitivity and $v_0 / (1 + d_j/\tau_d)$ ($v_0=0.90, \tau_d=120\text{h}$) captures urgency (shorter interactive jobs exhibit higher relative aversion to queue delay per billed GPU-hour).
